Google reviews for dentists: how to get more (and stay compliant)

Google reviews are one of the most powerful tools a dental practice has for attracting new patients, but collecting them the wrong way can put your GDC registration at risk. This guide explains exactly how to build a strong review profile compliantly and consistently.

For most people searching for a new dentist, the decision is made before they ever pick up the phone. They look at Google, scan the star ratings, read a handful of reviews, and move on. If your practice has a thin review profile or a handful of outdated entries, you are losing patients to competitors who have simply done a better job of asking. The frustrating part is that the solution is not complicated, but it does need to be done carefully. Google reviews for dentists sit at the intersection of patient care, marketing, and professional regulation, and getting it wrong can cost you far more than a few lost enquiries.

Why your Google review count is probably lower than it should be

Most dental practices underperform on Google reviews for one simple reason: they never ask. The patient has a great appointment, the clinician is pleased with the outcome, the reception team waves them off cheerfully, and then nothing happens. No prompt, no follow-up, no reminder. That patient goes home, gets on with their life, and the opportunity is gone.

There are a few reasons this happens so consistently in dental settings. First, the clinical team often feels awkward asking for reviews directly, worried it might seem unprofessional or self-promotional. Second, front-of-house staff may not have a reliable system or script. Third, many practices simply have no automated follow-up process in place. The result is that the patients who do leave reviews tend to be the ones who had a problem, because dissatisfaction is a much stronger motivator than satisfaction.

This creates a skewed profile. A practice might have hundreds of happy patients and a handful of difficult interactions, yet the review profile only reflects the difficult ones. That is a marketing problem, a reputation problem, and, increasingly, a local search ranking problem.

The compliance picture: what the GDC and ASA actually say

Before you set up any review-gathering system, you need to understand the rules. Dental professionals in the UK are regulated by the General Dental Council, and the advertising of dental services is subject to ASA rules and the CAP Code. Neither body publishes a specific clause that says "thou shalt not ask for reviews," but the principles that apply to testimonials and patient communications are directly relevant.

What you must not do

  • Offer incentives for reviews. Asking patients to leave a review in exchange for a discount, a free product, or any other benefit is not acceptable. Google's own policies prohibit it, and from a professional standards perspective, it risks creating a misleading impression of your services.
  • Selectively suppress negative reviews. Only directing satisfied patients to Google while quietly discouraging unhappy ones from posting publicly is known as "review gating." It breaches Google's terms of service and creates a dishonest public record.
  • Respond to reviews in a way that confirms clinical details. If a patient mentions their treatment in a review, your response must not confirm, deny, or elaborate on any clinical information. Doing so could breach patient confidentiality, even if the patient themselves disclosed it first.
  • Fabricate or solicit fake reviews. This should go without saying, but purchasing reviews or asking staff or friends to post them is fraudulent and in direct conflict with GDC standards of honesty and integrity.

What you are entirely permitted to do

  • Ask all patients, without filtering by satisfaction, to share their experience on Google.
  • Send a follow-up message after an appointment with a direct link to your Google review page.
  • Respond to reviews professionally and warmly, without breaching confidentiality.
  • Display reviews on your website, provided you do not cherry-pick in a misleading way.

The key principle is honesty. Your review profile should be a genuine reflection of patient experience, gathered fairly and systematically.

Building a compliant review-gathering system that actually works

The practices that build strong Google review profiles consistently are not doing anything clever or underhand. They have simply systematised the ask and made it easy for patients to respond. Here is what that looks like in practice.

Timing your request correctly

The best moment to ask for a review is shortly after a positive interaction, when the patient is still engaged. For most practices, this means sending an automated follow-up message between two and twenty-four hours after the appointment. Long enough for the patient to be home and settled, short enough that the experience is fresh.

Your patient management software almost certainly has the ability to trigger these messages. If it does not, a simple text or email workflow can be set up through third-party tools that integrate with most practice management systems.

Making the ask easy

The biggest barrier to leaving a review is friction. If a patient has to search for your practice on Google, find the review section, and work out how to post, most of them will not bother. A direct link to your Google review form removes almost all of that friction. You can generate this link from your Google Business Profile dashboard and include it in your follow-up message.

Keep the message short. Something along the lines of: "Thank you for visiting us today. If you have a moment, we would really appreciate it if you could share your experience on Google. Here is a direct link." That is all it needs to be.

Training your front-of-house team

Automated messages work well, but a brief verbal mention at the end of the appointment reinforces the request. Train your reception team to mention reviews naturally as part of the sign-off conversation. It does not need to be a sales pitch. A simple "we do rely on patient feedback quite a lot, so if you are happy to leave us a Google review that would be brilliant" is enough. Most patients are glad to help a practice they like.

Responding to every review

Responding to reviews, positive and negative, signals to both patients and Google that your practice is active and engaged. For positive reviews, a short, warm thank-you is sufficient. For negative reviews, respond calmly, acknowledge the concern, and invite the patient to contact you directly to resolve the matter. Never argue, never reveal clinical details, and never become defensive in public. Your response is read by every prospective patient who finds that review, not just the person who wrote it.

How this connects to your wider local SEO performance

Google reviews are not just a reputation tool. They are a local search ranking signal. Google's algorithm for local results, the pack of three listings that appears when someone searches "dentist near me" or "NHS dentist Sheffield," takes review quantity and recency into account. A practice that has collected a significant number of recent, genuine reviews consistently outperforms one with the same number of reviews spread over five years.

This means your review strategy should be ongoing, not a one-off campaign. Practices that run a push to collect reviews and then stop tend to see their relative position erode as competitors continue to gather new ones.

Where most practices go wrong, and what a specialist agency does differently

The most common mistake practices make is treating Google reviews as a one-time task rather than an ongoing system. They run a burst of activity, collect twenty or thirty reviews, and then let the process lapse. Six months later, the review profile looks stale, and the impact on search visibility begins to fade.

A second common mistake is managing the Google Business Profile in isolation, without connecting it to the broader marketing and SEO strategy. Reviews are one signal among many, and they work best when the rest of your local search presence is optimised alongside them.

Working with a specialist dental marketing agency changes this in a few specific ways. First, the review-gathering process is integrated into your existing patient communication workflow from the outset, so it runs consistently without relying on anyone remembering to do it. Second, response management can be handled on your behalf, ensuring replies are professional, timely, and compliant with GDC guidance on patient confidentiality. Third, your review performance is tracked as part of a wider local search dashboard, so you can see how it connects to visibility, traffic, and enquiry volumes over time.

Compliance is built into the process from the start, rather than retrofitted after something goes wrong. For practices in regulated industries, that matters more than most agencies appreciate.

A practical next step

If your practice has fewer than thirty Google reviews, or if your most recent review is more than two months old, that is worth addressing now. The good news is that a well-run system can produce meaningful results within a few weeks, without any incentives, without any manipulation, and without any compliance risk.

Start by auditing your current profile: how many reviews do you have, what is the average rating, how recently were they posted, and are you responding to them? Then look at whether your practice management system can support automated follow-up messages, and whether your front-of-house team has a clear, confident way to mention reviews at the end of appointments.
