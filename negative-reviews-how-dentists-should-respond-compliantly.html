Negative reviews: how dentists should respond (compliantly)

A single poorly handled negative review can do more damage than the original complaint. This guide explains how UK dental practice owners can respond to online criticism compliantly, professionally, and in a way that actually builds trust.

Every dental practice receives a negative review eventually. Whether it is a genuine complaint about waiting times, an unfair one-star post from a patient who never actually attended, or something far more serious involving clinical care, the moment you see it, the temptation is to react quickly and defensively. That temptation is understandable, but it is also one of the most common and costly mistakes practice owners make. Responding to dental reviews without a clear, compliant approach can turn a minor inconvenience into a regulatory problem, a reputational crisis, or both. This guide walks you through exactly what to do instead.

Why negative reviews feel so high-stakes (and why they are)

Your Google Business Profile, your NHS or private directory listings, and platforms like Trustpilot or Doctify are often the first thing a prospective patient sees before they even visit your website. A pattern of unanswered or poorly handled negative reviews signals to that prospect that complaints are ignored. Equally, a practice that responds with visible frustration, clinical detail, or dismissiveness can look far worse than the reviewer.

The stakes are elevated further because dentistry is a regulated profession. The General Dental Council (GDC) expects registrants to handle complaints professionally and in line with their Standards. The Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) has jurisdiction over public-facing promotional content, and a response to a review is, in many respects, a piece of public-facing content. This is not a space where improvised responses are wise.

The most common mistakes practice owners make

Disclosing clinical information in a public reply

This is the most serious error. When a patient leaves a negative review, you may feel you have context they have omitted. Perhaps their complaint relates to a treatment outcome you carefully documented and discussed. The instinct to correct the record publicly is natural, but sharing any clinical detail, treatment history, or appointment information in a public reply is almost certainly a breach of patient confidentiality, regardless of whether the patient has already disclosed that information themselves. Your duty of confidentiality does not disappear because a patient has chosen to post publicly.

Denying or dismissing the complaint

Telling a reviewer they are mistaken, or that their experience does not reflect your practice, rarely lands well with the audience reading it. Even if the review is factually inaccurate, a combative or dismissive reply tends to confirm the reviewer's characterisation of the practice rather than refute it.

Offering refunds or compensation in a public reply

Any offer of remedy should happen through a private channel. Publicising it risks creating expectations, attracting opportunistic complaints, and can complicate your professional indemnity position. Always take that conversation offline.

Simply not responding

Silence is itself a response. A negative review that sits unanswered for weeks tells prospective patients that you either did not see it or did not care enough to address it. Neither impression is one you want to create.

What a compliant, effective response actually looks like

The goal of any public response is not to win an argument with the reviewer. It is to demonstrate to the wider audience, the people researching your practice right now, that you take concerns seriously and handle them professionally. Keep that audience in mind with every word you write.

Acknowledge without admitting

You can express that you are sorry to read that someone has had a disappointing experience without accepting liability for a specific clinical outcome or conceding that the account is accurate. Phrases such as "we are sorry to read you felt this way" or "we take all feedback seriously" are not admissions. They are demonstrations of professionalism.

Invite offline resolution

Every public response should direct the reviewer to a named point of contact, typically your practice manager, with a telephone number or email address, and invite them to get in touch so the matter can be looked into properly. This shows prospective patients that you have a complaints process and are willing to use it. It also moves the conversation to a setting where you can speak freely and document the exchange.

Keep it brief

Two to four sentences is usually sufficient. Lengthy replies often over-explain or inadvertently introduce new detail. Brevity signals confidence.

Do not incentivise removal

Offering a patient something in exchange for removing or amending their review may constitute a breach of platform terms and could be viewed as misleading by the ASA. Address the underlying complaint through your practice complaints procedure instead.

A template you can adapt

The following is a general framework. You will need to adapt it to the specific circumstances and ensure it aligns with your practice's complaints policy. It is not legal or regulatory advice, and you should always seek guidance from your indemnifier if a review touches on a serious clinical matter.

"Thank you for taking the time to leave feedback. We are sorry to read that your experience did not meet the standard we aim to provide. We take all concerns seriously and would welcome the opportunity to look into this further. Please contact [Name], our Practice Manager, on [phone number or email], and we will be happy to assist."

That is it. No clinical detail, no defence of a treatment decision, no dismissal of the complaint. Just a professional acknowledgement and a clear next step.

What about fake or malicious reviews?

Not every negative review reflects a genuine patient experience. Occasionally, reviews are posted by individuals who never attended the practice, by competitors acting in bad faith, or by someone targeting the wrong listing by mistake. In these cases, you can report the review to the platform and request removal, but this process can be slow and success is not guaranteed.

In the meantime, your public response should still follow the same principles: brief, professional, and inviting contact. Stating that you have no record of the reviewer's details as a patient, without naming or identifying anyone, is generally acceptable and signals to prospective patients that the review may not be genuine. Avoid language that is aggressive or accusatory, even if the review almost certainly is.

Why your wider online presence makes a difference

One negative review is far less damaging when it sits alongside a strong volume of recent, genuine positive reviews. Practices that invest consistently in asking satisfied patients to share their experience, through compliant, non-incentivised requests, build a review profile that puts the occasional criticism in context.

This is also where your broader digital presence matters. A well-structured, professionally maintained website that ranks well in local search results means that when someone searches your practice name after seeing a review, they encounter a credible, authoritative presence rather than a thin or outdated site. Local SEO for dental practices is a significant part of managing how your practice appears online, not just in terms of visibility but in terms of the full picture a prospective patient forms before they pick up the phone.

Responding to dental reviews is, in other words, only one part of your online reputation management. The review response handles one data point. A coherent digital strategy handles the whole picture.

A final thought

Negative reviews are not the threat most practice owners believe them to be. A practice with forty positive reviews and two thoughtfully handled negative ones often looks more credible than a practice with ten perfect reviews and nothing else. Authenticity and professionalism are what patients are looking for, and a measured, compliant response to criticism demonstrates both.
