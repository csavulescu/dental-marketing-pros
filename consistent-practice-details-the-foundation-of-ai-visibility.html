Consistent practice details: the foundation of AI visibility

By Cristian Savulescu, Founder · Published 20 November 2026

If your practice name, address or phone number appears differently across directories and websites, AI assistants may simply overlook you. Here is what to do about it.

When a prospective patient asks ChatGPT, Google's AI Overviews, Gemini, Copilot or Perplexity to suggest a dental practice nearby, those systems do not browse the web in real time the way a human would. They draw on structured and unstructured data they have already processed, cross-referencing multiple sources to build a picture of which businesses exist, where they are, and whether the information about them appears reliable. If your practice name, address or phone number appear in different formats across different platforms, that picture becomes blurred. The result is not a penalty, as such. It is something quieter and more damaging: AI systems may simply pass over your practice when compiling a recommendation, because the data does not resolve cleanly into a single, trustworthy entity. This is the core problem that NAP consistency for dental AI is designed to solve.

What NAP consistency actually means

NAP stands for Name, Address and Phone number. The principle is straightforward: every mention of your practice across every platform should be identical, not just broadly similar. That means the same legal or trading name, the same address format including postcode, and the same phone number including the area code.

In practice, it rarely works out that way. A practice that opened several years ago may have changed its name after a rebrand, moved premises, acquired a new phone number, or changed ownership entirely. Each of those events tends to leave a trail of outdated listings scattered across the web. Even practices that have never moved often find inconsistencies, because different directories have different input fields and their own house styles. One might abbreviate "Street" to "St", another might drop the building name, another might list a mobile number that was used during a renovation years ago.

For traditional local search engine optimisation, NAP inconsistency has long been considered a trust signal problem. But the stakes are higher in an AI-influenced search landscape, because the systems involved are attempting to reason about your practice rather than simply rank a page.

Why AI assistants are sensitive to conflicting data

It is worth being clear about what we know and what we do not. The precise ranking factors used by large language models and AI search systems are not publicly documented in the way that, say, Google's core algorithm updates are discussed in their developer documentation. Any agency claiming to know exactly how ChatGPT or Gemini weights one source over another should be treated with scepticism.

What we can say with reasonable confidence is this: AI systems that generate local or business recommendations draw on a range of data sources. These include Google Business Profile, structured data on websites, established directories such as NHS Choices, Yell, Thomson Local and professional registers, local business citations, and the content of a practice's own website. When those sources agree with each other, the AI has a strong basis for describing your practice accurately. When they conflict, the AI faces an ambiguity it cannot easily resolve.

In some cases, conflicting information may cause an AI to hedge or omit a recommendation altogether. In others, it may surface incorrect details, which can lead to patient frustration even when you are mentioned. Neither outcome is good for a practice trying to build a sustainable new-patient pipeline.

This is not a niche technical concern. As more patients in the UK begin using AI assistants as part of their search behaviour, particularly for healthcare decisions where they want a confident, trustworthy answer rather than a list of links to sift through, the quality of your underlying data becomes a more significant commercial factor. The common sources of NAP inconsistency in dental practices

Legacy directories and aggregator platforms

Many directories automatically pull data from each other or from older datasets. A listing created in 2015 on one platform may have been replicated across dozens of aggregators without anyone at the practice being aware of it. These "ghost listings" can persist for years and may carry outdated addresses, old practice names or disconnected phone numbers.

Google Business Profile versus the practice website

It is surprisingly common to find that the address format on a Google Business Profile does not match the address on the contact page of the practice website. One might include the county, the other might not. One might spell out the full practice name including "Dental Practice", the other might use a shortened trading name. Both sources are likely to be consulted by AI systems, so discrepancies between them carry particular weight.

NHS and professional registration data

Practices registered with NHS England or listed on the NHS website carry a high degree of authority as a data source. If the name or address format used there differs from what appears elsewhere, it creates a conflict between a highly trusted source and your own marketing presence. This is worth auditing carefully.

Social media profiles

Facebook, Instagram and LinkedIn business pages are increasingly indexed and referenced by AI systems. A practice that has updated its website and Google Business Profile after a move or rebrand may have forgotten to update its social profiles, leaving a conflicting record in a prominent place.

How to audit and correct your NAP data

A thorough NAP audit begins with establishing a canonical version of your practice details. Before you can fix anything, you need to agree internally on the exact name, address and phone number format that should appear everywhere. Write it down and treat it as a reference standard.

From there, the audit involves searching for your practice name and address across the major directories, aggregators, review platforms and professional registers. The key platforms for UK dental practices include Google Business Profile, NHS website, Bing Places, Apple Maps, Yell, Thomson Local, Checkatrade, Trustpilot, Facebook, and any dental-specific directories you have used in the past. You should also search for your practice name in combination with your old address or old phone number if either has changed.

Where you find inconsistencies, the correction process involves claiming or verifying the listing if you have not already done so, then updating the details to match your canonical record. Some platforms allow immediate self-service edits. Others require a verification process, a written request, or in some cases a phone call. The process is time-consuming and not glamorous, but it is foundational to everything else you do in terms of local and AI-influenced visibility.

Beyond the listings themselves, your own website should use structured data markup, specifically LocalBusiness schema, to present your NAP details in a machine-readable format. This gives AI systems a clear, authoritative source that you control directly. The schema should match your canonical record exactly and should be present on your homepage and contact page at minimum.

Why specialist agency support tends to produce better results

It is entirely possible to audit and correct your own NAP data, particularly if your practice has not changed name, address or phone number and has only been operating for a few years. For many practices, however, the picture is more complicated than it first appears.

Practices that have been trading for a decade or more, that have rebranded, changed ownership, relocated or expanded to multiple sites, often have a surprisingly large footprint of inconsistent or duplicated listings. Finding them all requires systematic tools and experience, not just a manual search. Knowing which platforms carry the most weight for AI data sources, and in what order to prioritise corrections, is also the kind of practical knowledge that comes from working across a wide range of practices over time.

Beyond the correction process itself, a specialist agency brings ongoing monitoring. NAP data does not stay fixed. Aggregators update their datasets, directories create new listings based on old information, and ownership changes at the platform level can reset or overwrite verified data. What looks clean today may need attention again in six months.

There is also the broader context of GEO strategy. NAP consistency is a foundation, but it works alongside other factors that can help AI systems understand your practice more fully: the depth and quality of content on your website, the authority of the domains that mention you, the presence of structured data, and the coherence of your practice's digital footprint as a whole. Addressing NAP in isolation, without considering how it connects to those other elements, limits how much ground you can gain. Our GEO services take this joined-up view from the outset.

A practical starting point

If you are not sure where your practice currently stands, the most useful first step is a structured audit. Check your Google Business Profile against your website contact page. Search for your practice name on the NHS website and compare the details. Run a quick search for your old address or phone number if either has changed. These three checks alone will often surface the most significant inconsistencies.

From there, work through the major directories methodically, correct what you find, and implement LocalBusiness schema on your website if it is not already in place. Document everything you change and keep a record of which platforms you have verified, so that future audits are faster.
