Should your dental practice be on TikTok? An honest take

TikTok is no longer just a platform for teenagers, but that doesn't automatically make it right for every dental practice. This article helps you decide honestly, and shows you how to use it well if you do.

Every few months, a new platform arrives and the advice from every corner of the internet is the same: your business needs to be on it, immediately. TikTok has generated more of this noise than almost anything before it. If you are a dental practice owner in South Yorkshire or North Derbyshire trying to make sensible decisions about where to invest your time and marketing budget, that noise is not helpful. What you need is a straight answer. So here it is: TikTok can work for dental practices, but only under specific conditions, and for most practices right now it is not the first place to focus. This article explains when it makes sense, how to do it properly if you decide to go ahead, and what the real pitfalls look like in a regulated profession.

Why Dental Practices Are Confused About TikTok

The confusion is understandable. TikTok's growth over the last few years has been significant, and its audience is no longer exclusively under twenty-five. A meaningful proportion of its active users in the UK are now in their late twenties, thirties, and forties, which overlaps with the demographic that books cosmetic treatments, Invisalign, and implants. At the same time, you have probably seen competitors or practices in other cities posting videos and apparently getting large amounts of engagement, and it is natural to wonder whether you are missing out.

The problem is that engagement on TikTok and new patient enquiries are two very different things. A video of a dentist doing a satisfying composite restoration might collect a hundred thousand views, but the majority of those viewers are scattered across the country or the world, and most of them have no intention of booking an appointment. Reach without relevance does not fill appointment books.

There is also a compliance dimension that is often ignored in the general enthusiasm. Dental marketing in the UK is regulated by the GDC and the ASA, and the informal, off-the-cuff nature of TikTok content makes it very easy to post something that inadvertently breaches advertising standards or professional guidelines, particularly around before-and-after imagery, treatment claims, and patient consent.

When TikTok Actually Makes Sense for a Dental Practice

Rather than treating this as a yes-or-no question, it helps to think about whether your practice meets a specific set of conditions. TikTok is most likely to generate genuine return if the following are true.

Your practice focuses on cosmetic or elective treatments

TikTok is a discovery platform. People scroll it passively and encounter content they were not looking for. This means it works well for creating awareness of treatments that people did not know they wanted or did not realise were accessible to them. Composite bonding, teeth whitening, Invisalign, smile makeovers, and similar treatments fit this model well. NHS-focused or purely general practices will find the return much harder to justify because the audience is not in a mindset to be persuaded, they are already registered with a practice or they are searching Google when they have a dental need.

Someone in your team is willing and able to appear on camera consistently

TikTok rewards consistency and personality. Static images and text-heavy posts do not perform. The practices that build genuine followings have a recognisable person at the centre, usually a dentist or treatment coordinator, who appears regularly, speaks naturally on camera, and communicates in a way that feels human rather than corporate. If nobody in your practice is willing to do this, producing content that performs well will be an uphill struggle regardless of the budget you spend on production.

You have a strategy that integrates TikTok with the rest of your marketing

TikTok in isolation rarely converts. Practices that see real results use it as part of a wider approach: driving awareness through TikTok, capturing interest through a well-structured website and landing pages, and converting enquiries through a reliable follow-up process. If your website is not built to convert visitors, or your phone team is not equipped to handle cosmetic enquiries, adding TikTok to the mix will not fix those gaps. It is worth reviewing your full marketing services picture before committing to a new channel.

The Compliance Risks You Cannot Afford to Ignore

This is the section most TikTok guides skip, and it is the most important one for dental practices.

The GDC's standards apply to all communications, including social media. The ASA applies the CAP Code to paid promotions and, in practice, to content that is clearly commercial in nature even if it is organic. The specific risks on TikTok include the following.

  • Before-and-after imagery: The ASA has taken action against cosmetic businesses for using before-and-after images in ways that create misleading impressions of typical results. On TikTok, where transformation content performs extremely well, the temptation to post this kind of content is high. Patient consent must be properly documented, and the presentation must not imply that any patient can expect the same outcome.
  • Testimonials and endorsements: Paid partnerships and gifted treatments must be disclosed clearly. Even organic content that features a patient talking positively about their experience needs to be handled carefully in terms of consent, accuracy, and whether it could constitute a misleading endorsement under the CAP Code.
  • Treatment claims: Describing a treatment as painless, guaranteed to work, or producing a specific outcome is problematic under both GDC guidance and ASA rules. Informal video content makes it easy to say something that would never appear in your written marketing but that still creates a regulated claim.
  • Promotion of prescription-only treatments: Any content that touches on treatments requiring a prescription, or that could be interpreted as promoting such treatments to the general public, must be handled with particular care.

None of this means TikTok is off-limits. It means it requires the same level of compliance awareness as any other marketing channel, and probably more active oversight given how quickly content is produced and published on the platform.

What Good TikTok Content Looks Like for a Dental Practice

If you have decided that TikTok is worth pursuing, here is what tends to work within the constraints of a regulated profession.

Education-led content

Short videos explaining what a treatment involves, what the process looks like, or how to choose between options perform well and carry lower compliance risk than transformation content. A sixty-second video answering the question "what actually happens during an Invisalign consultation" is genuinely useful, naturally positions your practice as expert, and does not require you to make claims about outcomes.

Day-in-the-life and behind-the-scenes content

Content that shows your team, your environment, and the personality of your practice builds familiarity and trust. Patients choose dentists partly on instinct, and seeing real people in a real practice helps reduce the anxiety that stops many people from booking. This kind of content is relatively straightforward to produce and does not carry significant compliance risk.

Myth-busting and FAQs

Addressing common concerns directly, whether that is about pain, cost, or the results of a specific treatment, positions you as a trustworthy source and attracts people who are already considering treatment but have reservations. This format suits TikTok's conversational style and tends to generate genuine engagement from people who are closer to a booking decision.

Consistency over virality

One viral video will not transform your new patient numbers. Regular, reliable posting, even if individual videos get modest engagement, builds an audience over time and keeps your practice visible to people who are in the early stages of considering treatment. Trying to engineer virality usually produces content that feels inauthentic and rarely converts anyway.

Why Doing This Properly Is Harder Than It Looks

The practices that struggle most with TikTok are not the ones who tried it and stopped. They are the ones who post sporadically when someone on the team has a spare five minutes, with no clear strategy, no compliance check, and no understanding of how TikTok's algorithm works. This produces content that neither performs well nor moves people down a decision-making path toward booking.

Doing it properly requires a clear content plan, an understanding of what performs on the platform without crossing compliance lines, someone who can appear on camera consistently, a process for obtaining and documenting consent when patients are involved, and a way of connecting TikTok activity back to genuine enquiries and bookings. That is a meaningful operational commitment, and it is one that many practice owners underestimate when they decide to give it a go.

The Honest Verdict

TikTok for dentists is not a shortcut, and it is not the right starting point for every practice. If your website is not converting visitors, if your Google presence is weak, or if your existing marketing channels are not working well, those are higher priorities. TikTok amplifies what is already working; it rarely fixes what is not.

If you run a cosmetic-focused practice, have the team and the appetite for consistent video content, and are willing to operate with proper compliance oversight, TikTok is a legitimate channel worth exploring. It can build genuine awareness among people who are not yet searching but who are exactly the kind of patients you want to attract.

The key is going in with realistic expectations, a proper plan, and an understanding of the rules specific to your profession.
