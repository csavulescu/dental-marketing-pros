How to lower your cost-per-click on dental Google Ads

Google Ads can be one of the most effective ways to attract new patients, but overspending on clicks quietly erodes your return. This guide explains exactly why dental practices pay too much per click and what you can do to fix it.

If you are running Google Ads for your dental practice and your cost per click feels uncomfortably high, you are not alone. Dental keywords are among the most competitive in local search advertising, and without careful management, budgets can disappear faster than they should. The good news is that a high cost per click is rarely fixed. In most cases, it is a symptom of specific, correctable problems in your account setup, targeting, or ad quality. Understanding what drives the price up is the first step towards bringing it down.

Why Dental Clicks Cost So Much in the First Place

Google Ads operates on an auction system, but the price you pay per click is not determined by your bid alone. Google assigns every ad a Quality Score based on three factors: expected click-through rate, ad relevance to the search query, and landing page experience. Advertisers with higher Quality Scores pay less per click for the same position. Advertisers with low Quality Scores pay a premium.

In competitive markets like Sheffield, Rotherham, Chesterfield or Doncaster, many dental practices are bidding on the same broad terms. When those accounts are poorly structured, with generic ad copy and weak landing pages, Quality Scores drop across the board and the entire auction becomes more expensive. The practices with well-optimised accounts consistently pay less per click and appear more often. If your account falls into the first category, you are effectively subsidising your competitors.

The Most Common Reasons Your Cost Per Click Is Too High

1. Broad or Poorly Matched Keywords

Using broad match keywords like dentist or dental treatment without tight controls is one of the fastest ways to waste budget. Broad match tells Google to show your ad for loosely related searches, many of which will never convert into patient enquiries. You end up paying for irrelevant clicks, your click-through rate suffers, your Quality Score drops, and your cost per click rises.

The fix is to audit your search terms report regularly and use a tighter mix of phrase match and exact match keywords, supplemented by a robust negative keyword list. For a South Yorkshire dental practice, negative keywords might include terms like free NHS dentist if you are a private practice, or names of competitor towns outside your catchment area.

2. Low Quality Scores Across Your Ad Groups

A Quality Score below 6 out of 10 is a warning sign that you are paying more than necessary. Low scores usually trace back to one of two problems: ad copy that does not closely reflect the keyword being searched, or a landing page that feels generic and slow to load.

Improving your Quality Score is one of the most direct ways to lower cost per click on dental ads. Start by tightening your ad groups so that each one covers a narrow theme, for example, one ad group for teeth whitening, another for Invisalign, another for dental implants. Write ad headlines that include the specific keyword for that group. Then ensure the landing page the ad points to matches that same theme precisely, with relevant copy, a clear call to action, and fast load times on mobile.

3. Poor Account Structure

Many dental practices, particularly those who set up their own campaigns or inherited an agency's legacy account, have a single campaign with all keywords bundled together. This structure makes it impossible to write highly relevant ads for each keyword, which suppresses Quality Scores and inflates costs.

A better approach is to build tightly themed ad groups, or in some cases separate campaigns, for each core service. This allows you to control bids individually, write specific ad copy, and direct traffic to the right landing page. It takes more time to set up correctly, but the effect on average cost per click can be meaningful over weeks and months.

4. Ignoring Device, Location and Time Bid Adjustments

If you are running the same bids across all devices, times of day, and locations, you are likely overpaying for clicks that rarely convert. For most dental practices, the majority of valuable enquiries come from mobile users searching locally during daytime hours. Paying the same rate for a desktop click at midnight is unlikely to represent good value.

Bid adjustments let you increase bids where your data shows strong conversion rates and reduce them where it does not. Review your campaign data by device, by hour of day, and by geographic radius around your practice. Even modest adjustments in the right direction can reduce your effective cost per click without reducing visibility when it matters most.

5. Weak or Missing Ad Extensions

Ad extensions, now called assets in Google Ads, do not cost extra to use, but they increase the size and relevance of your ad, which improves click-through rates. A higher click-through rate feeds directly into a better Quality Score, which reduces your cost per click over time.

Most dental practices should be using sitelink extensions pointing to specific service pages, call extensions showing your practice phone number, location extensions linked to your Google Business Profile, and callout extensions highlighting relevant points such as same-day appointments or convenient parking. If these are missing from your account, adding them is a straightforward way to improve performance at no additional cost per click.

The Role of Landing Pages in Your Cost Per Click

It is easy to focus purely on the ads themselves and overlook the page people land on. Google evaluates landing page experience as part of Quality Score, which means a slow, generic, or irrelevant landing page will cost you money even before a single visitor reads it.

For dental Google Ads, each main service should have its own dedicated landing page. That page should load quickly on mobile, make it easy for someone to book or enquire, and contain copy that matches the intent behind the search. Sending all your ad traffic to your homepage is one of the most common and costly mistakes in dental PPC, and correcting it often produces a noticeable improvement in both Quality Score and conversion rate.

How Ongoing Optimisation Keeps Costs Under Control

Lowering your cost per click is not a one-time task. Google Ads accounts require regular attention to maintain performance. Search behaviour shifts, competitors adjust their bids, and Google's algorithm updates continuously. An account that was well-optimised six months ago may have drifted significantly since.

Effective ongoing management includes weekly search term reviews to add new negative keywords, regular A/B testing of ad headlines and descriptions, monitoring Quality Score changes across ad groups, reviewing conversion data to identify which keywords are genuinely generating enquiries rather than just clicks, and adjusting bids in response to seasonality. Implant and teeth-straightening enquiries, for example, often follow seasonal patterns that are worth accounting for in your bidding strategy.

A Practical Checklist to Start Lowering Your Cost Per Click Today

  • Review your search terms report and add irrelevant queries to your negative keyword list.
  • Check the Quality Score for each keyword in your account and identify those scoring below 6.
  • Ensure each ad group covers a single, tightly defined theme rather than a broad mix of services.
  • Audit your landing pages. Each service ad group should point to a relevant, fast-loading page.
  • Check that all relevant ad extensions are active and up to date.
  • Review your bid adjustments by device, time of day, and location, and use your conversion data to guide them.
  • Set a regular weekly or fortnightly schedule for account review rather than leaving campaigns to run unattended.

None of these steps requires a large budget increase. In many cases, the practices that most successfully lower cost per click on dental ads do so by spending more carefully on the same budget, not by increasing it.
