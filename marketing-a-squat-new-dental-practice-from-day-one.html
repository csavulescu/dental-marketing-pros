Marketing a squat (new) dental practice from day one

Opening a squat dental practice is one of the most financially significant decisions a dentist can make, and your marketing plan is just as critical as your fit-out. This article sets out a clear, stage-by-stage approach to building a patient base from the moment you unlock the door.

Opening a squat dental practice puts you in an unusual position: you have a fully equipped surgery, a team ready to go, and a diary with nothing in it. Unlike buying an established practice, there is no inherited patient list, no word-of-mouth reputation built over years, and no goodwill to fall back on. From day one, every single patient you see has to be actively attracted. That reality makes new dental practice marketing not a nice-to-have, but the most important operational priority you have in your first twelve months.

This article explains why so many squat practices struggle to gain traction early on, what the underlying causes tend to be, and how a structured, properly sequenced marketing approach changes the outcome. It is written for practice owners and principal dentists who want practical guidance, not vague reassurances.

Why squat practices often underperform in their first year

The most common reason a new practice fails to fill its books quickly is not the quality of its dentistry. It is a combination of late-stage marketing, inconsistent messaging, and over-reliance on a single channel.

Many practice owners spend the bulk of their pre-launch energy on CQC registration, fit-out, recruitment, and equipment. Marketing becomes something to "sort out once the doors are open." By that point, weeks or months of potential awareness-building have already been lost. Patients in your catchment area have no idea you exist, your Google Business Profile has zero reviews, and your website has been indexed by search engines so recently that it carries almost no authority.

The second problem is channel confusion. A practice owner who has never run a marketing budget before will often spread spend thinly across a Facebook page, a leaflet drop, a local newspaper ad, and perhaps a basic website, none of which are connected by a consistent message or tracked in any meaningful way. Budget disappears without a clear picture of what is working.

Third, and this is specific to squat practices, there is no existing reputation to amplify. Every marketing channel you use is starting from zero simultaneously, which means the sequencing of your activity matters enormously.

The fix: a staged approach to new dental practice marketing

Stage one: lay the digital foundations before you open

Your website, your Google Business Profile, and your social media presence should all be active before your first patient walks through the door. This is not about generating bookings before you are ready; it is about giving Google and your local community time to register that you exist.

A well-structured website built around local search terms, for example "dentist in [your town]" or "NHS dentist accepting patients in [area]", takes time to gain traction in organic search results. The earlier you launch it, the earlier that clock starts. Your Google Business Profile should be claimed, fully completed with your opening hours, services, location, and photographs, and verified as far in advance as possible.

During the pre-launch period, use your social channels to document the fit-out, introduce your team, and explain your ethos. This costs nothing beyond time and creates genuine local interest before you have even opened. It also gives you a small but engaged following to communicate with when you do launch.

Stage two: generate immediate visibility with paid search

Organic search rankings take months to build. In the meantime, paid search advertising on Google gives you immediate visibility to people who are actively looking for a dentist in your area right now. A well-managed Google Ads campaign targeting local search terms can put your practice at the top of results from your first week of trading.

The critical word there is "well-managed." Poorly configured paid search campaigns waste budget at an alarming rate. Broad match keywords, weak ad copy, and landing pages that do not convert visitors into enquiries are extremely common problems. For a squat practice with a finite launch budget, those mistakes are expensive. This is one area where working with people who understand both dental services and paid advertising pays for itself relatively quickly.

Stage three: build local reputation fast

Reviews are the currency of local trust. A practice with no reviews, regardless of how good its website looks, will lose patients to a nearby competitor with forty positive ratings. Your review-gathering process should begin with your very first patient.

This does not mean pressuring patients or incentivising reviews, both of which fall outside GDC and ASA guidelines. It means having a straightforward, consistent follow-up process: a post-appointment message thanking the patient and inviting them to share their experience on Google if they are happy to do so. Over time, this compounds significantly. A practice that gathers reviews consistently from day one will have a meaningful profile within its first few months.

Local reputation-building also extends offline. Introducing yourself to neighbouring businesses, engaging with community events, and connecting with local community groups on social media all contribute to the kind of grassroots awareness that paid advertising alone cannot replicate.

Stage four: invest in local SEO for sustainable growth

Once your practice is operational and your paid campaigns are running, the medium-term priority is improving your organic search visibility. Local SEO for dental practices involves a combination of on-page optimisation, consistent name-and-address information across online directories, Google Business Profile activity, and the gradual accumulation of quality content on your website.

A common mistake is treating SEO as a one-off task rather than an ongoing activity. Publishing a handful of pages and then leaving your site untouched will not move the needle. Regular, relevant content, whether that is information about treatments you offer, answers to questions patients commonly ask, or updates about the practice, signals to search engines that your site is active and worth ranking.

For squat practices in competitive towns and cities, local SEO is the difference between a practice that is discoverable and one that relies entirely on paid advertising indefinitely. The goal is to reduce your cost per acquisition over time as organic traffic grows.

Stage five: convert interest into registered patients

Attracting enquiries is only half the job. How your team handles those enquiries determines whether you convert a potential patient into a registered one. A well-designed marketing strategy accounts for this: it ensures that the journey from clicking on an ad or finding your website to booking an appointment is as smooth as possible.

This means a website that loads quickly on mobile devices, a clear and prominent call to action, an online booking option where possible, and a telephone manner that reflects the warmth and professionalism of your practice. If enquiries are falling through the cracks because calls go unanswered or the booking process is clunky, no amount of marketing spend will compensate for it.

How a specialist dental marketing agency does this better

A generalist marketing freelancer or a generic digital agency can build a website and run ads. What they typically cannot do is bring sector-specific knowledge to the work: understanding GDC advertising guidelines, knowing which treatment terms carry the highest patient intent in search, recognising the difference in how NHS and private patients search and behave, and benchmarking your results against other dental practices rather than other industries.

For a squat practice, the stakes of getting this wrong are higher than for an established practice. You do not have a cushion of existing patients. Every pound of your marketing budget needs to work efficiently from the start, and your marketing activity needs to be sequenced and coordinated rather than improvised.

A note on budget and expectations

It would be dishonest to suggest that marketing a squat practice from zero is quick or cheap. Building a full patient list takes time even with an effective strategy in place. What a well-executed plan does is compress that timeline and reduce the proportion of your budget that is wasted. It also gives you visibility into what is working so that you can make informed decisions about where to invest as your practice grows.

The practices that tend to struggle are those that either delay marketing activity until the financial pressure is already acute, or those that spend inconsistently without a clear plan. Starting early, with a coherent strategy and realistic expectations, makes a measurable difference to how quickly a squat practice reaches sustainability.
