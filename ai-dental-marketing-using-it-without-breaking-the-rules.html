AI & dental marketing: using it without breaking the rules

By Cristian Savulescu, Founder · Published 21 September 2026

AI tools are changing how dental practices market themselves, but using them carelessly can put you on the wrong side of GDC and ASA regulations. This article explains what to watch for and how to use AI responsibly in your marketing.

Artificial intelligence has moved from novelty to everyday tool faster than most industries expected, and dental marketing is no exception. Practice owners across South Yorkshire and North Derbyshire are already using AI to write website copy, generate social media posts, draft email newsletters, and even suggest paid advertising text. The appeal is obvious: it is faster, cheaper, and requires no brief-writing or agency call. But here is the problem that rarely gets mentioned in the enthusiasm. AI does not know the GDC's ethical advertising standards. It does not know the ASA's CAP Code. It does not know that in dentistry, the wrong word in the wrong place can lead to a formal complaint, a costly investigation, and lasting reputational damage. AI dental marketing compliance is not a niche concern reserved for large corporate groups. It is a live issue for any practice that is already using, or is considering using, AI in its marketing output.

Why AI and Dental Advertising Rules Are a Difficult Combination

The core tension is this: AI language models are built to produce confident, persuasive, conversion-focused text. Dental advertising regulations exist precisely to limit persuasion that could mislead patients or exploit their anxieties. Those two objectives pull in opposite directions.

The GDC's Standards for the Dental Team and its associated guidance on advertising make clear that dental professionals must not make claims that are misleading, exaggerated, or that create unrealistic expectations. The ASA's CAP Code applies the same standards to non-broadcast advertising, including websites, social media, and paid digital ads. Taken together, these rules prohibit a wide range of language that AI tools produce almost automatically, including claims about being "the best" practice in an area, guaranteed outcomes from treatments, before-and-after imagery used in misleading ways, and testimonials that imply results a patient should expect.

AI does not produce these things maliciously. It produces them because that is what high-converting marketing copy typically looks like in unregulated industries. The model has been trained on a vast range of marketing material, most of which was never subject to healthcare advertising standards. When you ask it to write a landing page for dental implants, it will do so with the same confident, outcome-focused tone it would use for a software product or a gym membership. The output sounds polished. It may even sound authoritative. But it will frequently contain statements that breach GDC guidance or CAP Code provisions without a single sentence that flags the problem.

The Specific Risks You Need to Know About

Outcome-Based Claims

AI-generated copy frequently includes phrases such as "restore your confidence," "transform your smile permanently," or "guaranteed results." These are not minor stylistic choices. The GDC guidance on advertising states that claims must be accurate and not create false impressions. Implying permanence for a treatment with known longevity limitations, or framing a cosmetic outcome as guaranteed, can constitute a misleading claim under both GDC standards and the CAP Code. If a patient makes a complaint citing marketing language, that copy can be treated as a promise and assessed against the actual outcome they received.

Comparative and Superlative Statements

AI tools default to competitive framing. "South Yorkshire's leading implant centre," "the most trusted dentist in Sheffield," and similar phrasings are generated routinely. Under the CAP Code, comparative advertising claims must be substantiated. If you cannot prove, objectively and verifiably, that you are "the most trusted," you cannot say it. AI will not ask you for that proof before drafting the line.

Before-and-After Content

AI tools that generate image prompts or social media plans will frequently suggest before-and-after content for cosmetic treatments. The ASA has been explicit in recent years that such imagery must not mislead patients about the likely outcome of treatment. The imagery itself is not automatically prohibited, but the context, captions, and any implied expectations must be handled carefully. AI-generated captions for such images are rarely written with this nuance in mind.

Patient Testimonials

AI can draft testimonials in seconds. This should be an immediate red flag. Under CAP Code rules, testimonials used in advertising must be genuine, must reflect the honest opinion of the person named, and must not be used selectively to create misleading impressions. Fabricated or AI-generated testimonials are not compliant. Even genuine testimonials need careful handling if they include outcome-based language.

Pricing and Offers

Promotional copy for discounted treatments or payment plans needs to comply with CAP Code pricing rules. AI-generated promotional copy will often omit required qualifications, fail to include terms and conditions, or present price comparisons in ways that are not substantiated.

Why This Problem Is Getting Worse, Not Better

The volume of AI-generated content is increasing rapidly, and the pace at which it is produced makes review difficult. A practice owner who uses an AI tool to draft a week's worth of social media posts in twenty minutes is unlikely to spend an equivalent amount of time checking each post against GDC guidance and the CAP Code. The speed that makes AI attractive is also the reason compliance review gets skipped.

There is also a confidence problem. AI output tends to read well. It is grammatically correct, flows naturally, and uses the kind of language that feels authoritative. This surface quality reduces the likelihood that a busy practice owner will scrutinise it as carefully as a rougher first draft might prompt them to. The better the AI gets at producing plausible text, the harder it becomes to spot the compliance issues embedded in it.

The GDC has not yet issued specific guidance on AI-generated dental marketing. That silence should not be read as permission. The existing standards apply regardless of whether content was written by a human, an agency, or an AI model. The practice owner remains responsible.

The Fix: A Compliance-First Approach to AI in Dental Marketing

None of this means AI tools are off the table. Used correctly, with appropriate oversight, they can genuinely reduce the time and cost of producing marketing content. The key is building a compliance checkpoint into every stage where AI is involved.

Use AI for Structure, Not for Claims

AI is effective at drafting outlines, suggesting topic angles, reformatting existing copy, and handling repetitive tasks such as generating meta descriptions or structuring FAQs. It is less reliable when asked to make specific claims about treatments, outcomes, or your practice's standing. Use AI as a drafting aid, not as the final authority on what your marketing should say.

Build a Checklist Specific to GDC and CAP Code Rules

Before any AI-generated content is published, it should be reviewed against a written checklist that includes the following questions. Does the copy make any claim about guaranteed outcomes? Does it use superlatives that cannot be substantiated? Does it include testimonials, and if so, are they genuine and appropriately caveated? Does any pricing copy include all required qualifications? Would a reasonable patient read this and form an expectation that the treatment cannot reliably deliver?

This checklist does not need to be long. It needs to be used consistently and by someone with enough knowledge of the relevant rules to apply it meaningfully.

Keep a Compliance Log

Document the review process for your marketing content. If a complaint is ever made, being able to demonstrate that your content was reviewed against GDC and ASA standards before publication is a meaningful form of protection. A short note recording who reviewed each piece of content and when is far better than no record at all.

Brief AI Tools with Compliance Parameters

When using AI tools, include explicit parameters in your prompts. State that the content must comply with GDC advertising standards, must avoid outcome guarantees, must not use unsubstantiated superlatives, and must not include fabricated testimonials. AI tools will not apply these constraints automatically, but they will follow them when instructed. This does not replace a compliance review, but it reduces the volume of problems that need to be corrected at the review stage.

How a Specialist Dental Marketing Agency Approaches This Differently

Working with a dental marketing agency that understands the regulatory landscape changes the nature of the compliance risk significantly. At Dental Marketing Pros, the approach to AI in content production is built around the same GDC and ASA standards that govern your practice's advertising obligations. AI tools are used where they add genuine value, specifically for research, structural drafting, and workflow efficiency. The output is reviewed by people who understand both what good dental marketing looks like and what the regulatory framework prohibits.

This matters because compliance is not just about avoiding complaints. It is about building marketing that patients trust and that reflects well on your practice over time. Misleading copy, even if it never generates a formal complaint, damages the relationship between your marketing and the patients it reaches. When you look at the range of marketing services built specifically for dental practices, compliance is embedded in the process rather than treated as an afterthought.

The pace of change in AI tools is not slowing, and the gap between what AI can produce and what GDC-compliant dental marketing requires is unlikely to close without deliberate, expert input. General marketing agencies without dental sector experience will not apply the right filters. In-house teams without regulatory training face the same gap. A specialist agency removes that exposure and lets you focus on the clinical side of your practice.

The Bottom Line for Practice Owners

AI is a useful tool. It is not a compliant one by default. Every piece of AI-generated content that goes out under your practice's name is your responsibility, and the GDC does not make exceptions for content produced by an algorithm. The fix is not to avoid AI altogether. It is to use it with the right oversight, the right brief, and the right expertise reviewing the output before it reaches patients.

If you are unsure whether your current marketing, AI-generated or otherwise, is sitting within the boundaries the GDC and ASA set, that uncertainty is worth resolving sooner rather than later.
