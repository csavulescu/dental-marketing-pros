Setting a marketing budget for a small dental practice

By Cristian Savulescu, Founder · Published 12 October 2026

Most small dental practices either underspend on marketing and wonder why new patients are scarce, or scatter budget across channels with no clear plan. This article gives you a practical framework for budgeting that actually reflects how dental marketing works.

Most small dental practice owners approach their marketing budget one of two ways: they spend as little as possible and hope word-of-mouth does the rest, or they react to whichever agency or platform salesperson contacted them most recently. Neither approach is a plan. The result is either chronic underinvestment that keeps the practice quiet, or a trail of invoices for things that never quite worked. Setting a small dental practice marketing budget should not be guesswork, and this article will show you a more structured, defensible way to do it.

Why small practices struggle to budget for marketing

The core problem is that dental school does not teach business finance, and marketing spend feels inherently risky compared with a new piece of equipment you can see and touch. A dental chair has an obvious function. A Google Ads campaign or an SEO retainer feels abstract, and when results are slow to materialise, the temptation is to cut the budget entirely.

There is also a genuine lack of benchmarks. Large private or mixed practices have more data to work with and often employ a practice manager with some marketing literacy. A smaller single-site or two-surgery practice is often run by the principal dentist, who is also the clinician seeing patients all day. Time and mental bandwidth are short, and marketing decisions get made reactively rather than strategically.

A third factor: many practices have no reliable way of knowing which marketing activity is actually generating new patients. Without that visibility, it is impossible to know whether to spend more, less, or differently. If you have not yet looked at how phone calls from your ads are being tracked and attributed, call tracking for dental ads: stop wasting budget on lost calls is worth reading before you go any further with paid activity.

The fix: a percentage-of-revenue starting point

One of the most practical ways to anchor your marketing budget is to work from a percentage of your gross revenue. A commonly used range for service businesses in competitive local markets is somewhere between five and ten percent of annual turnover, with newer or growing practices often sitting closer to the higher end.

For a small UK dental practice turning over, say, £400,000 to £600,000 per year, that suggests a marketing budget in the range of £20,000 to £60,000 annually, or roughly £1,700 to £5,000 per month. That will feel like a wide range, and it is, because the right figure genuinely depends on your situation: how competitive your local area is, whether you are trying to fill a new practice or grow an established one, and which treatments you are focusing on.

If you want to see how the numbers scale as a practice grows, the dental marketing budget breakdown for a £1M practice shows how channel allocation shifts at a higher revenue level and gives useful context even if you are not there yet.

Do not confuse activity with investment

One mistake that consistently undermines small practice budgets is treating marketing spend as a cost centre rather than an investment. A piece of equipment depreciates. Good marketing, done correctly, builds compounding returns: an optimised website keeps attracting patients long after the initial build cost is paid for, a strong local reputation reduces the cost of paid acquisition over time, and a healthy patient list with high lifetime value changes the economics of every campaign you run.

Speaking of which: if you have never calculated the lifetime value of a patient at your practice, it is one of the most useful exercises you can do before setting any budget. Lifetime patient value: the metric that changes your ad budget explains why this single number should inform how much you are willing to spend to acquire one new patient.

How to allocate your budget across channels

Once you have a total figure in mind, the next question is where it goes. There is no single correct answer, but there is a logical hierarchy for a small dental practice in a local market.

Start with the foundations

Before spending a penny on paid advertising, your foundational digital presence needs to be in good shape. That means a well-structured, fast-loading website optimised for local search, a fully completed and actively managed Google Business Profile, and consistent name-address-phone information across directories. If these are weak, paid traffic will convert poorly and you will waste budget.

Foundational work is not free, but it should be relatively stable expenditure once it is done properly. A one-off investment in a professionally built, conversion-optimised website, followed by ongoing SEO management, is typically a good use of a portion of your budget because the returns accumulate over time.

Paid search for faster results

If you need new patients in the short term, paid search (primarily Google Ads) is the most direct route for most practices. You can target people actively searching for a dentist or a specific treatment in your area, and you only pay when someone clicks. The trade-off is that the moment you stop paying, the traffic stops.

For a small practice, a realistic starting point for Google Ads in a moderately competitive area such as South Yorkshire or North Derbyshire might be between £500 and £1,500 per month in media spend, not including management fees. In more competitive areas or for high-value treatments such as implants or Invisalign, the spend required to achieve meaningful volume will be higher.

Social media: realistic expectations

Social media has value for dental practices, primarily for brand awareness and patient retention rather than direct acquisition. Organic social posts rarely generate significant new patient enquiries on their own, but they contribute to trust and recognition when a prospective patient is deciding between practices. Paid social, particularly Meta ads, can work well for aesthetic treatments where visual results are compelling.

As a rough guide, social media should rarely be the largest line item in a small practice budget unless you have a specific, well-defined campaign reason for prioritising it.

A realistic monthly budget breakdown for a small practice

  • SEO and local search management: £500 to £1,000 per month
  • Google Ads media spend: £500 to £1,500 per month
  • Google Ads management: £250 to £600 per month
  • Website maintenance and content: £150 to £400 per month
  • Social media and reputation management: £150 to £400 per month

This puts a working total somewhere between £1,550 and £3,900 per month before any larger one-off projects. These are indicative ranges, not guarantees of what you will pay or what you will get. Local market conditions, competition, and your specific goals all affect the right figure.

Where practices go wrong with their budgets

Beyond underspending, there are a few consistent patterns that lead small practices to waste what they do spend.

Spreading too thin. Trying to be active on every channel with a modest budget means none of them get the investment they need to work properly. It is better to do two or three things well than six things inadequately.

Stopping too soon. SEO in particular takes time to show results. Practices that invest for three months, see nothing dramatic, and then cancel have often done so just as the groundwork was beginning to pay off. Paid search can deliver results faster, but it also requires ongoing testing and refinement.

No measurement framework. If you cannot attribute new patients to specific channels, you cannot make rational budget decisions. Every practice should, at minimum, be tracking where enquiries come from, whether through call tracking, form analytics, or asking new patients directly at registration.

Conflating marketing spend with agency fees. Some practice owners think of their agency retainer as "the marketing budget." The agency fee is the cost of expertise and management. The media spend is a separate and additional cost. Confusing the two leads to underinvestment in the channels themselves.

How a specialist agency changes the equation

A generalist marketing agency can build websites and run social media for any type of business. A dental-specialist agency brings a different kind of value: familiarity with GDC and ASA advertising regulations, experience of what converts in dental specifically, and an understanding of the patient journey from first search to booked appointment.

That sector knowledge means less time and budget wasted on approaches that do not suit the dental context, and more confidence that your marketing is compliant. It also means benchmarks. A specialist agency working with multiple practices in your region has a realistic sense of what CPCs (cost per click) look like locally, what conversion rates are achievable from well-run campaigns, and where your budget will have the most impact.

Taking the next step
