Location pages for dental practices: how to do them properly

By Cristian Savulescu, Founder · Published 30 September 2026

Dental location pages are one of the most misunderstood parts of practice SEO. This guide explains what goes wrong, why it happens, and how to build location pages that genuinely rank and convert.

If your dental practice serves patients from more than one town, or if you want to rank for specific neighbourhoods and nearby areas, location pages should be a core part of your website. Most practices either do not have them at all, or they have versions that are so thin and repetitive that Google treats them as near-duplicate content and largely ignores them. The result is that you are invisible to people searching for a dentist in the very postcodes you want to reach. This article explains why dental location pages so often fail, what a genuinely effective one looks like, and how a specialist agency approaches the work differently.

What dental location pages are supposed to do

A location page is a standalone page on your website dedicated to a specific geographic area. It signals to search engines that your practice is relevant to searches made by people in that area, even if your surgery is a few miles away. For a practice in Chesterfield, for example, a well-built page targeting Staveley or Dronfield can help you capture patients who would otherwise never find you.

Done properly, these pages serve two audiences at once. They tell search engines that you understand, serve and are relevant to a particular community. And they tell prospective patients from that area that you are a realistic, convenient option, not just a generic dental practice that happens to exist nearby.

When location pages work, they expand the geographic footprint of your practice website without requiring you to open a second surgery. That is a significant return on a relatively modest investment in content.

Why most dental location pages fail

Thin content with no local substance

The most common mistake is creating pages that say almost nothing. A typical poor example reads: "Looking for a dentist in [Town]? Our practice is conveniently located near [Town] and offers a full range of NHS and private treatments. Call us today." That is roughly forty words, none of which tell Google anything useful or give a prospective patient any reason to trust you. Google's quality guidelines have become increasingly precise about what constitutes a genuinely helpful page, and content like that consistently underperforms.

Copy-pasted across every page

The second failure is duplication. A practice wants to target eight nearby towns, so they create eight pages using the same template and simply swap the place name. Google identifies these pages as near-duplicate content. At best, only one of them ranks. At worst, they dilute each other's authority and none of them performs well. This approach actually makes your SEO position weaker, not stronger.

No relevance signals for the specific area

Effective dental location pages need to contain content that is genuinely specific to that community. Not just the town name repeated several times, but references to local geography, transport links, nearby landmarks, the kinds of patients who typically travel from that area, and any particular context that makes the page feel written for real people from that place. Without this, a page cannot meaningfully compete against a practice that is physically based in that town and has a Google Business Profile there.

Missing the technical foundations

Location pages also fail when the technical basics are absent. No unique title tag. No localised meta description. No structured data to help search engines understand the page's geographic relevance. No internal links from the rest of the site to give the page authority. These are not optional refinements; they are the minimum requirement for a location page to have any chance of ranking.

What a properly built dental location page contains

A meaningful, specific introduction

The page should open with a paragraph that a person from that area would read and recognise as written for them. References to well-known local roads, the town centre, nearby GP surgeries, or recognisable landmarks all contribute to this. It does not need to be long, but it needs to be real. Generic phrases like "conveniently situated" and "close to local amenities" carry no weight whatsoever.

Relevant treatment content

Which treatments are people from this area most likely to be searching for? If you offer Invisalign and teeth whitening and you are targeting a commuter village where a high proportion of residents are working professionals, lead with those. If you are targeting an area with a significant NHS waiting list problem, your NHS availability or access-focused messaging belongs on that page. The content should reflect what patients from that specific community are likely to want.

Practical patient information

How do people from this area actually get to your practice? Driving time, bus routes, parking availability, and whether you are near a train station all matter to real patients making a decision. This kind of detail also adds local relevance signals that generic content cannot replicate.

Trust-building elements

Testimonials, GDC registration details, accreditations, and a clear explanation of your NHS or private fee structure all belong on a location page as much as they belong on any other part of your site. Patients searching from a specific area are making exactly the same decision as any other new patient, and they need the same reassurance. On that note, it is worth thinking carefully about how you handle pricing information across your site; our article on pricing pages for dental treatments: should you show prices explores the trade-offs in detail.

Correct technical implementation

Each location page needs a unique title tag that includes both the primary treatment or service and the location. The meta description should be written to earn a click from someone who is comparing several results. The page should include LocalBusiness schema markup, and it should be linked to from your site's navigation or from a dedicated locations hub page. Getting this right from the start is far easier than retrofitting it later.

Where location pages fit within your wider site structure

Location pages do not exist in isolation. They need to be connected logically to the rest of your website, both for users navigating between pages and for search engines crawling your site's structure. A practice with multiple locations faces a more complex version of this challenge, and the decisions you make about site architecture affect how well every individual page performs.

If you run more than one surgery, the structural considerations become particularly important. Our article on multi-location dental websites: structuring for several practices covers how to handle this without creating a site that undermines itself through internal competition between pages.

For practices with a single surgery that simply wants to reach patients from surrounding areas, a well-planned navigation structure that surfaces location pages without burying them is essential. How a specialist dental marketing agency approaches location pages differently

There is a meaningful difference between a general digital marketing agency building location pages and a team that works exclusively in dental. A generalist will often rely on a templated approach, because it is faster and cheaper to produce. The problem is that Google has become increasingly effective at identifying templated content, and templated location pages rarely generate the organic rankings that justify the effort of creating them.

A specialist agency starts with keyword research that is specific to each target area, identifying how people in that community actually search for dental services. It considers what treatment demand looks like in that postcode, what competitors are currently ranking for, and what gaps exist in the local search landscape. From that research, each location page is built with a distinct brief, not a shared template with a find-and-replace on the town name.

The technical side is handled properly from the outset: unique metadata, appropriate schema, internal linking that distributes authority from your strongest pages to the newer location pages, and a crawlable URL structure that makes sense both to users and to search engines. Our dental SEO service covers all of this as part of a coherent strategy rather than a standalone tactic.

It is also worth noting that location pages, like every other part of your public-facing website, sit within a regulatory framework. The GDC and ASA have clear requirements about how dental services are described and promoted online. Claims need to be accurate, substantiated and not misleading. A good agency builds this compliance awareness into the content process from the beginning, rather than treating it as an afterthought. While you are thinking about the legal dimensions of your site, it is worth reviewing our guide to website legal pages every dental practice needs.

The realistic outcome of getting this right

Well-built dental location pages, properly implemented and maintained, can meaningfully extend the geographic reach of your practice website. They will not produce results overnight, and no reputable agency will promise you specific rankings or a guaranteed number of new patients. What they do is give your practice a genuine, sustained presence in areas where you currently have none, and they do so in a way that compounds over time as the pages build authority.

The alternative is what most of your competitors are already doing: thin, duplicated pages that Google largely ignores, leaving local search results to be dominated by whoever has invested in doing this properly.
