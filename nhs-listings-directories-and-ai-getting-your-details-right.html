NHS listings, directories and AI: getting your details right

By Cristian Savulescu, Founder · Published 27 November 2026

Outdated or inconsistent directory listings don't just frustrate patients — they confuse AI assistants that now influence which practices get recommended. This article explains what goes wrong, why it matters more than ever, and how to fix it.

If your practice details are listed differently across NHS.uk, Google Business Profile, Yell, and a dozen other directories, you have a problem that goes well beyond minor inconvenience. As AI assistants such as ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews, Gemini, Copilot and Perplexity become a more common starting point for people searching for a dentist, those inconsistencies are being picked up, repeated and sometimes amplified. A patient who asks an AI tool "find me an NHS dentist accepting new patients near Sheffield" is relying on that tool to give accurate information. If your data is a mess, the tool may ignore your practice entirely, present outdated details, or worse, present a competitor instead.

This is not a niche technical concern. It is a practical, fixable business problem. And yet the majority of practice owners we speak to have never done a thorough audit of how their details appear across the directories that matter most.

Why directory data is so often wrong

It is worth understanding how the problem starts, because it rarely comes from carelessness. Most practices set up their listings when they first opened or took over, and then life got in the way. A phone number changed. The practice rebranded. Opening hours shifted after a staffing change. NHS availability opened and closed. Each of those events creates a fork between what is real and what is published, and those forks multiply across every directory that holds your data.

The NHS website is particularly important here. NHS.uk draws patient-facing information from the NHS Digital database, and updating it requires going through the correct NHS channels rather than simply editing a profile as you would on Google. Many practices do not realise this, so their NHS listing continues to show old hours, an old phone number, or an incorrect statement about whether they are accepting new patients. That listing then feeds other aggregators, which feed comparison tools, which feed AI systems that are trying to assemble an accurate picture of your practice from whatever public data they can find.

Google Business Profile adds another layer of complexity. Third parties can suggest edits to your profile, and Google sometimes applies those suggestions automatically. Unless you are actively monitoring your profile, your address, phone number or category could change without you knowing. The same applies to Bing Places, Apple Maps, and several of the dental-specific directories that syndicate data between themselves.

Why this matters more now that AI is involved

Search behaviour is shifting. A meaningful and growing proportion of people, particularly younger adults, now use AI assistants as their first port of call when looking for local services. These tools do not simply return a list of links. They synthesise information from multiple sources and present a confident-sounding answer. The critical point is that they are only as reliable as the data they draw from.

When an AI assistant tries to answer "which dental practices near me are taking NHS patients", it is likely drawing on NHS.uk, Google data, dental directories, and whatever other authoritative sources it can access. If your NHS listing says you are not accepting patients but your Google profile says you are, the AI has a conflict it must resolve, often by defaulting to caution or by omitting your practice from the response altogether. If your phone number on Yell differs from your phone number on the NHS site, that inconsistency signals unreliability, even if both listings are for a perfectly well-run practice.

The article Consistent practice details: the foundation of AI visibility goes into more depth on this dynamic, but the short version is this: AI tools reward consistency and penalise contradiction. That is not a ranking factor you can game. It is simply a reflection of how these systems try to give users trustworthy information.

For a worked example of how this plays out in a specific local market, the piece on AI search for dentists in Huddersfield: getting recommended locally is worth reading alongside this one, as the same principles apply whether your practice is in Huddersfield, Chesterfield, or anywhere else across the UK.

The key directories to audit

Not every directory carries the same weight, but the following are worth prioritising because they are the most likely to be referenced by AI tools and by traditional search engines.

  • NHS.uk: The most authoritative source for NHS dental practices. Accuracy here matters enormously, both for patients and for the systems that pull from it.
  • Google Business Profile: Heavily used by Google AI Overviews and directly influences map pack visibility. Monitor it actively, not just occasionally.
  • Bing Places and Apple Maps: Often overlooked, but Copilot (which uses Bing data) and Siri (which uses Apple Maps) draw from these sources when answering local queries.
  • Dental-specific directories: Sites such as Dentist Finder, Find-a-Dentist tools run by regional NHS bodies, and private directory aggregators. Some of these syndicate to others, so getting them right at source matters.
  • Yell, Thomson Local and general business directories: Lower priority individually, but collectively they contribute to the broader data landscape that AI systems reference.
  • Your own website: Your NAP (name, address, phone number) details on your website, particularly in the footer and on the contact page, serve as the reference point against which everything else should align.

What getting it right actually involves

A thorough fix is a multi-step process, not a single afternoon's work. Here is what it looks like in practice.

Step one: a full audit

Before you can fix anything, you need to know what is out there. That means searching for your practice name and address across all the directories listed above, noting every discrepancy, and creating a master record of your correct details. This master record should include your registered practice name exactly as it appears with the GDC, your full address in a consistent format, your main phone number, your website URL, your opening hours for every day of the week, and your current NHS availability status. Everything else should match this record.

If you would like a head start, a free SEO audit from a specialist team can surface the most damaging inconsistencies quickly, giving you a prioritised list rather than requiring you to investigate everything yourself.

Step two: NHS database corrections

Updating your NHS.uk listing is not as straightforward as editing a Google profile. Changes need to go through your NHS England area team or, in some cases, through the NHS Digital team directly, depending on what needs to be changed. This is worth doing carefully and following up, because confirmation that a change has been accepted does not always mean it has propagated through to the patient-facing website immediately.

Step three: claim and verify every listing you can

For directories that allow you to claim ownership of your listing, do so. A claimed listing gives you control over updates and usually carries more trust with the platform. Google Business Profile verification is essential. Bing Places and Apple Maps Business Connect are both free to set up and worth doing.

Step four: build structured data into your website

Adding LocalBusiness schema markup to your website gives AI tools and search engines a machine-readable version of your practice details directly from the most authoritative source: you. This can help reduce the reliance on third-party data and reduce the chance of an AI tool pulling conflicting information. Schema is technical to implement correctly, but the investment is worthwhile.

Step five: establish a review cycle

Directory accuracy is not a one-off fix. It needs to be reviewed every time anything changes at the practice, and periodically checked even when nothing has changed, because third-party edits and data feed errors can introduce new problems. Building this into your practice's quarterly admin routine is a sensible approach.

A final note on AI: what it can and cannot do with your data

It is worth being clear-eyed about what AI assistants are doing when they reference dental practices. These tools are not accessing a live database that you can directly update. They are drawing inferences from publicly available information, and their outputs vary depending on the query, the tool, and the moment. That means no one can guarantee your practice will be cited in an AI-generated response, and any agency that tells you otherwise is not being straight with you.

What you can do is make your practice's data as accurate, consistent and well-structured as possible across the sources AI tools are most likely to reference. That can help your practice appear more reliably in relevant responses, and it certainly reduces the risk of a patient being given wrong information if your practice does come up. Those are meaningful, achievable goals.
