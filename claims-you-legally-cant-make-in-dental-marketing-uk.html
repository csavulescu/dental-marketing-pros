Claims you legally can't make in dental marketing (UK)

By Cristian Savulescu, Founder · Published 17 August 2026

Many UK dental practices unknowingly publish marketing claims that breach GDC and ASA guidelines, putting their registration and reputation at risk. This guide explains exactly where the lines are drawn and how to stay the right side of them.

Dental marketing in the UK operates inside a tighter regulatory framework than most practice owners realise. The General Dental Council, the Advertising Standards Authority, and the Committee of Advertising Practice all have a say in what you can and cannot publish, whether that is a Google Ad, a social media post, or a line on your own website. The problem is that many of the phrases that feel natural in marketing, words like "the best," "guaranteed," or "pain-free," are precisely the ones that can trigger a formal complaint, a mandatory correction, or worse. This article sets out the most common categories of non-compliant dental marketing claims, explains why practices keep making them, and shows what good, compliant copy looks like in practice.

Why Non-Compliant Claims Keep Appearing in Dental Marketing

It is rarely deliberate. Most practices that publish problematic marketing copy do so because they have taken inspiration from a competitor's website, asked a non-specialist copywriter to draft their content, or simply repeated phrases that have been used in the industry for years. The regulatory landscape has tightened considerably over the past decade, and material that passed without comment some years ago now attracts ASA rulings.

There is also a genuine confusion between what feels true and what can be substantiated. A dentist who has been placing implants for fifteen years may genuinely believe their results are outstanding. That belief, however, does not automatically make the claim "the best implant results in Sheffield" permissible under CAP Code rules. Substantiation must be objective, verifiable, and held before the claim is made.

Claims the GDC Standards for the Dental Team Prohibit

The GDC's Standards for the Dental Team (Standard 4) requires registrants to justify public trust in the profession. In the context of marketing, this translates into several clear prohibitions.

Superiority claims without evidence

Phrases such as "South Yorkshire's leading dental practice," "number one for dental implants," or "the most experienced cosmetic dentist in the region" are superiority claims. Under ASA and GDC guidance, every superiority claim must be backed by robust, objective evidence. Market research conducted by an independent body, verified patient volume data, or a formally awarded accreditation can support such a claim. A feeling of confidence, or even genuinely excellent patient feedback, cannot.

The fix is straightforward: replace unsubstantiated superlatives with specific, demonstrable facts. "Placing implants since 2008" is accurate and credible. "South Yorkshire's leading implant dentist" is not, unless you hold the data to prove it.

Claiming to be "GDC registered" as a differentiator

This one surprises many practice owners. Highlighting GDC registration as though it sets you apart from competitors implies that others are not registered, which is misleading. Every practising dentist in the UK is required to be GDC registered. Mentioning it in passing for transparency is acceptable; using it as a marketing advantage is not.

Claims the ASA CAP Code Flags as Misleading

The ASA applies the CAP Code to all non-broadcast advertising, which in practice means your website, social media, paid search ads, and email marketing. The most consistently problematic categories in dental advertising are as follows.

Outcome guarantees

Guarantees of clinical outcomes are impermissible. Dental treatments are medical procedures, and individual results vary. Claims such as "guaranteed white teeth in one session," "your implant will last a lifetime," or "zero pain during treatment" cannot be made responsibly. Even if the vast majority of patients experience a particular outcome, guaranteeing it to all patients is misleading because it cannot honestly be promised to every individual.

Compliant alternatives focus on process rather than outcome. "We use the latest whitening technology to achieve the best possible results for your teeth" communicates quality without guaranteeing a specific result. "Our team prioritises patient comfort throughout every procedure" speaks to care without promising a pain-free experience that cannot be guaranteed.

Before-and-after imagery used without proper context

Before-and-after photographs are widely used in cosmetic dental marketing, and they are not prohibited outright. However, the ASA requires that such images accurately represent what a typical patient can expect. Using a highly selected, exceptional result without acknowledging that outcomes vary, or presenting an image that has been digitally altered beyond basic colour correction, will breach the rules. Written consent from the patient, clear contextual copy, and accuracy in representation are all required.

Testimonials that include clinical claims

Patient testimonials are a powerful trust signal, but they must not contain clinical claims. A patient saying "I feel so much more confident since my treatment" is fine. A patient saying "My gum disease was completely cured" crosses into clinical territory that the practice cannot substantiate on behalf of an individual patient, and which may mislead prospective patients about the outcomes they should expect. Testimonials must be genuine, must not be incentivised in a way that biases them, and must not make efficacy claims the practice itself would not be allowed to make directly.

"Natural-looking" and similar subjective claims

Terms like "completely natural-looking," "invisible aligners," or "undetectable veneers" are more problematic than they appear. "Natural-looking" is a relative and subjective term; what looks natural to one patient may not to another, and the claim could be considered misleading if results vary. "Invisible aligners" is particularly common in clear aligner marketing and has attracted ASA attention because the aligners are not, in practice, invisible. "Discreet" or "virtually invisible in most cases" is a more defensible approach.

Price and Offer Claims: A Frequently Overlooked Risk Area

Promotional pricing in dental marketing is heavily scrutinised by the ASA. Several rules apply that many practices are unaware of.

  • A "was/now" price comparison is only valid if the higher price was genuinely charged for a meaningful period beforehand. Inflating the original price to make a discount look more impressive is a clear breach.
  • Free consultations must actually be free. If the consultation includes a cost that is recovered elsewhere, or if attendance creates a strong commercial pressure to purchase, this must be disclosed clearly.
  • Limited-time offers must genuinely be limited. Running the same "this month only" promotion every month is misleading under CAP guidance.

For practices running paid search campaigns, these rules apply with equal force to ad copy. A Google Ads campaign that includes a promotional claim in the headline must comply with all of the above, and the ASA has jurisdiction over paid digital advertising in exactly the same way it does over your website content.

How a Specialist Dental Marketing Agency Navigates These Rules

A non-specialist copywriter or a generalist digital agency will not typically know which dental marketing claims rules UK regulators apply, and they are unlikely to flag the risks in your current copy before publishing. The result is material that sounds plausible but exposes your practice to complaint, mandatory amendment, or reputational damage at the exact moment a prospective patient is forming an impression of you.

A specialist dental marketing agency works differently in several practical ways.

Copy is drafted with GDC and ASA guidance as a baseline, not an afterthought

Rather than writing naturally persuasive copy and then checking it for compliance, a specialist team starts from the compliance framework and builds persuasive, conversion-focused language within it. The two objectives are not in conflict; compliant copy can be compelling, specific, and effective. It simply requires a writer who knows where the boundaries are.

Ad campaigns are structured to convert without making impermissible promises

Paid search is one of the most common places practices publish non-compliant claims, because character limits create pressure to use punchy, superlative language. A specialist agency knows how to write dental PPC ads that are attention-grabbing and click-worthy without relying on claims that could prompt an ASA ruling or a Google policy violation.

Website content is audited before it compounds the problem

Most practices that have been trading for more than three or four years have accumulated content across multiple pages written at different times by different people. A compliance audit of your existing web copy, social profiles, and printed materials will almost always surface at least some language that does not meet current standards. Identifying and correcting it proactively is far preferable to doing so in response to a complaint.

The Practical Takeaway

The dental marketing claims rules in the UK exist to protect patients from misleading information, and that is a reasonable objective that most dentists would endorse. The challenge is that the boundary between persuasion and misleading claims is not always obvious, particularly for practice owners who are focused on clinical work rather than marketing regulation.

Audit your current website copy against the categories above. Look specifically for superlatives, outcome guarantees, price claim language, and any testimonials that stray into clinical territory. If you find any, change them before a competitor or a dissatisfied patient makes a formal complaint on your behalf.
