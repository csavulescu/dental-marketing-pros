Dental Google Ads match types explained

By Cristian Savulescu, Founder · Published 28 October 2026

Choosing the wrong Google Ads match types is one of the most common reasons dental practices bleed budget without seeing results. This guide explains how each match type works and how to use them strategically.

If you have ever run Google Ads for your dental practice and wondered why your budget disappeared without a corresponding rise in enquiries, match types are very likely part of the problem. Most practice owners who manage their own campaigns, or who have handed them to a generalist agency, are either using match types that are far too broad or sticking rigidly to exact match and missing patients who are actively searching for exactly what they offer. Getting this balance right is one of the highest-leverage decisions you can make in any dental PPC campaign.

What Are Google Ads Match Types and Why Do They Matter for Dentists?

Every keyword you add to a Google Ads campaign comes with a match type setting. That setting tells Google how closely a user's search query needs to resemble your keyword before your ad is eligible to appear. Choose too loosely and you pay for clicks from people who will never book with you. Choose too tightly and you miss a large proportion of the patients who would.

For dentists, the stakes are particularly high. Dental clicks are expensive relative to many other local service sectors, and a single wasted click that should have been blocked by a tighter match type, or a negative keyword, represents real money. Understanding the three main match types, broad match, phrase match and exact match, is therefore not a technical nicety. It is fundamental to running a campaign that is commercially worthwhile. If you are still weighing up whether paid search makes sense for your practice at all, it is worth reading Is Google Ads Worth It for Dentists? before going further.

The Three Match Types Explained

Broad Match

Broad match is the default setting in Google Ads, which is part of why it causes so many problems. When you add a keyword on broad match, Google will show your ad for searches that it considers semantically related to your keyword. That sounds helpful in principle, but in practice it means a keyword like dentist near me on broad match could trigger your ad for searches such as dental nurse jobs, teeth whitening at home kit or even queries about dental insurance that have nothing to do with booking an appointment.

Google has improved its broad match algorithm considerably in recent years, and its machine learning does get better at filtering irrelevant traffic over time, particularly when your campaigns have solid conversion tracking in place. However, for most dental practices, especially those with modest daily budgets, broad match remains risky without extremely thorough negative keyword lists and close monitoring. It is rarely the right starting point.

Phrase Match

Phrase match sits in the middle of the spectrum. Your ad will show when a user's search contains the meaning of your keyword, though additional words can appear before or after it. If your keyword is dental implants Sheffield on phrase match, your ad could appear for best dental implants Sheffield or affordable dental implants Sheffield city centre, but it is much less likely to appear for entirely unrelated queries.

Phrase match is often the workhorse of a well-structured dental campaign. It gives you meaningful reach while retaining enough control to keep irrelevant clicks to a minimum. The key is to combine it with a robust negative keyword list so that variations which are clearly off-target, such as queries about NHS waiting lists when you run a private practice, are excluded from the outset.

Exact Match

Exact match is the most restrictive option. Your ad shows only when the search query matches your keyword very closely, allowing for close variants such as plurals, misspellings and reorderings of the same words, but little else. A keyword like [emergency dentist Doncaster] in exact match will reach people searching precisely for that service in that location.

The upside is exceptional relevance and control. The downside is that search volume for any single exact match keyword can be low, which means you need a substantial list of exact match keywords to generate meaningful volume. Exact match works well for your highest-value, highest-intent services such as dental implants, Invisalign or same-day emergency appointments, where every click matters and conversion rates need to be as high as possible.

Why Getting Match Types Wrong Is So Costly for Dental Practices

The most common pattern is this: a practice sets up a campaign, adds keywords on broad match because it is the default, and within a few weeks has spent a significant portion of its budget on clicks that bear little relation to its services. The search terms report, which shows the actual queries that triggered ads, reveals searches from people looking for dental jobs, home whitening products, or patients in entirely different cities.

The second most common pattern is the opposite extreme. A practice, perhaps after a bad experience with broad match, switches everything to exact match and then wonders why the campaign barely spends and generates very few leads. With local dental keywords, search volumes can be relatively modest, and being too restrictive means you are invisible for the majority of the searches your potential patients are actually typing.

Budget wastage compounds quickly. You can learn more about how costs accumulate and what a realistic budget looks like in the article How much do Google Ads cost for a UK dental practice in 2026?, which puts the numbers in context.

How a Well-Structured Dental Campaign Uses Match Types

Start with Phrase and Exact Match for Core Services

A sensible structure for most dental practices is to use phrase match as the primary match type for service and location keyword combinations, and to layer in exact match for your highest-value terms. For example:

  • Phrase match: dental implants [city], Invisalign [city], emergency dentist [city]
  • Exact match: [dental implants Sheffield], [private dentist Rotherham], [emergency dentist open now]

This combination gives you reasonable volume from phrase match while using exact match to protect your budget on the most competitive, most expensive clicks.

Build Your Negative Keyword List Before You Launch

Negative keywords are what prevent your ads from showing on irrelevant searches. Without a thorough negative keyword list, even phrase match can trigger ads for queries you would never want to pay for. At the very minimum, a dental campaign should exclude terms such as:

  • Jobs, careers, vacancies, apprenticeship
  • NHS (if you are a private practice, or vice versa depending on your model)
  • At home, DIY, kit (for treatment-specific campaigns)
  • Free, cheap (unless these genuinely reflect your offering)
  • Veterinary, animal (dental searches can occasionally trigger these)
  • Competitor brand names (unless you are running a deliberate conquest campaign)

Negative keywords should be reviewed and expanded regularly using the search terms report. This is a task that gets neglected in self-managed campaigns and is one of the most tangible ways a specialist agency adds ongoing value.

Use Ad Groups to Align Match Types with Intent

Match types work best when your campaign structure mirrors how patients think about their problem. Group keywords by treatment and intent rather than throwing everything into one ad group. An emergency dental ad group will attract searchers with immediate, urgent intent, so it warrants tight match types and a highly specific ad and landing page. A cosmetic dentistry ad group may allow slightly broader phrase match coverage because patients in that category are often in a research phase and taking longer to decide.

This structure, sometimes called Single Keyword Ad Groups or tightly themed ad groups depending on your approach, ensures that the match type decision you make for one service does not inadvertently affect how your ads perform for another. For a fuller overview of how dental campaigns should be structured from the ground up, the Google Ads for dentists: a complete guide for UK practices is a practical reference point.

Introduce Broad Match Selectively and Only With Conversion Data

Once your campaign has been running for several months and you have reliable conversion tracking, there is a case for introducing broad match on a small number of keywords to discover new search terms you had not anticipated. Google's Smart Bidding algorithms perform considerably better when they have conversion data to work with, and broad match can surface intent that phrase and exact match miss entirely.

However, this should be done cautiously, with tight negative keyword controls already in place, and with a clear plan for reviewing the search terms report frequently. It is not a set-and-forget approach.

How a Specialist Dental Agency Manages Match Types Differently

A generalist agency or a self-managed campaign will often default to whatever Google recommends during campaign setup, which tends to favour broad match because it generates more impression volume and, from Google's perspective, more spend. A specialist dental agency approaches the question differently.

The starting point is understanding the commercial value of each treatment and the typical patient journey. Implants and orthodontics have long consideration cycles and high treatment values, which justifies a different match type strategy than emergency appointments, which are urgent and geographically specific. Match types are then set to reflect that intent, not to maximise clicks in the abstract.

Ongoing management matters as much as initial setup. Reviewing the search terms report weekly, expanding negative keyword lists, testing new phrase match variations and adjusting bids based on which match type combinations are driving actual bookings rather than just clicks: this is the difference between a campaign that improves over time and one that simply spends. You can see an example of how this kind of targeted, competitive approach works in practice in the article Dental Google Ads in Wakefield: outbidding bigger practices.

A Note on Google's Evolving Approach to Match Types

It is worth being aware that Google has been gradually expanding the reach of all match types over the past few years. What constituted phrase match behaviour in 2020 is not the same as it is today. Google has merged modified broad match into phrase match, and exact match now includes close variants that it did not previously. This means that even if you set up your campaign correctly some time ago, the actual behaviour of your keywords may have drifted. Auditing your search terms report against the match types you think you are using is a useful exercise for any practice that has not reviewed its campaigns recently.

Next Steps

Match types are one of those areas of Google Ads where a relatively small amount of knowledge, correctly applied, can make a meaningful difference to your cost per lead and the overall return on your ad spend. The principles are not complicated, but applying them well in the context of a dental campaign, with the right account structure, negative keyword discipline and ongoing review, takes time and experience.
